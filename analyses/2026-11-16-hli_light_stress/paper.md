# High-light-inducible (hli) genes in *Prochlorococcus*: light response or broad stress response?

## Question

*Prochlorococcus* MED4 carries 23 genes encoding small high-light-inducible proteins (HLIPs). We ask whether their transcripts respond to light, and whether they respond across stresses other than light. Where the available experiments allow it, we also ask whether that response is independent of light. The analysis is restricted to MED4 and is exploratory: it reuses published transcriptome experiments, several of which were inspected while the analysis was planned.

## Background

HLIPs are single-helix members of the chlorophyll a/b-binding protein superfamily. They are thought to protect the photosynthetic apparatus when absorbed light exceeds what photosynthesis can use: in *Synechocystis* PCC 6803, mutants lacking *hli* genes lose out to the wild type in high light and die at very high light (He et al. 2001), and HLIPs appear to take part in dissipating absorbed light energy as heat (Havaux et al. 2003). In that organism Hli levels rise not only in high light but also under nitrogen limitation, and some also under sulfur deprivation and low temperature (He et al. 2001), so a response beyond light is not unexpected. The *hli* gene family is greatly expanded in high-light-adapted *Prochlorococcus*: MED4 carries more than twice as many *hli* genes as the other cyanobacteria compared by Bhaya et al. (2002), some of them from recent duplications. Cyanophages carry *hli* genes that group with the multi-copy types found only in *Prochlorococcus*, and phage-mediated transfer may have contributed to the expansion (Lindell et al. 2004). MED4 has 23 *hli* genes. In the knowledge graph, 17 of them carry the chlorophyll a/b-binding domain (Pfam PF00504), five of these also the HliA–D-like domain (PF31213), and six have no domain annotation. Twelve lie next to another *hli* gene, in two tandem blocks of four and two pairs, and five pairs encode identical proteins. Several of the transcriptome studies used here name *hli* genes in their results (Steglich et al. 2006; Tolonen et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2011; Bagby & Chisholm 2015; Thompson et al. 2016); we compare their statements with our results in the Discussion. Because a cell under nutrient or CO2 limitation uses less of the light it absorbs, induction under such stresses could reflect either a light-driven photoprotective response or a broader stress response. The experiments brought together here comprise 27 MED4 transcriptome comparisons from 10 publications, covering light onset and photoperiod, photosynthetic electron transport, nitrogen, iron, CO2, salt and phage infection, together with non-stress comparisons.

## Methods

**Data sources and experiment selection.** All expression data were taken from a multi-omics knowledge graph that collects published *Prochlorococcus* transcriptome and proteome studies, together with their gene annotations and ortholog groups. We used every MED4 transcriptome comparison in the graph, from both microarray and RNA-seq platforms, and relied on each original study's own calls of which genes were significantly up or down. Proteome data were not used. One RNA-seq comparison, a 2016 study of MED4 in coculture with *Alteromonas* HOT1A3 (Aharonovich & Sher 2016), was excluded after inspection. In that table 82% of all log2 fold changes were negative, 291 genes with an absolute log2 fold change above 5 were nonetheless called not significant, and 18 distinct fold-change values were each shared by ten or more genes. This pattern is what one expects when many genes have near-zero read counts in one arm, so the table could not be read reliably. A phosphate-starvation microarray (Martiny et al. 2006) was also excluded: the knowledge graph holds only the 34 genes significant at 48 h, which include one *hli* gene, so the *hli* genes could not be assessed at the earlier time points and only weakly at 48 h. The remaining 27 comparisons come from 10 publications.

The *hli* gene set was defined as the 23 MED4 genes belonging to the 14 Cyanorak ortholog groups annotated as high-light-inducible proteins. Gene name, product description and ortholog-group membership all identify the same 23 genes.

**Grouping the experiments.** The comparisons differ in what they can say about light, so we grouped them before reading any result. Five *light-onset* comparisons, from a single study (Steglich et al. 2006), measure the response 45 min after cells kept 5 h in the dark were exposed to blue, green, red or white light (13 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹), or to brighter white light (55 µmol). All five share one dark control. A sixth comparison from the same study adds DCMU, which blocks electron flow out of photosystem II, to illuminated cells. Two *schedule-extension* experiments (Johnson et al. 2026) compare cells kept in light past their normal dusk, or in darkness past their normal dawn, with cells on their usual 12:12 h light–dark cycle sampled at the same time of day. Two experiments compare constant darkness with constant light, with and without phage infection (Thompson et al. 2016). A phage experiment from the same study, run entirely in the dark, is the one stress comparison in the collection that does not involve light.

The remaining comparisons are stresses applied in the light, or comparisons we treated as non-stress. The stresses are:
- nitrogen limitation, four experiments (Tolonen et al. 2006; Read et al. 2017; Weissberg et al. 2025);
- CO2 limitation, three experiments (Bagby & Chisholm 2015);
- phage infection in the light, two experiments (Lindell et al. 2007; Thompson et al. 2016);
- iron starvation (Thompson et al. 2011);
- low salinity (He et al. 2022).

The non-stress comparisons are:
- growth on urea or cyanate as the sole nitrogen source (Tolonen et al. 2006);
- re-addition of iron to starved cells (Thompson et al. 2011);
- air sparging compared with time 0 (Bagby & Chisholm 2015);
- coculture with *Alteromonas* (Weissberg et al. 2025).

For the 2025 coculture-versus-axenic experiment (Weissberg et al. 2025), the knowledge graph's design notes record that the arms are matched by growth state rather than by sampling day: the axenic control came from a separate repeat run sampled on days 7 and 14. On day 11 the coculture is compared with axenic cells on day 7, both growing exponentially, so that time point is a non-stress comparison. On day 18 it is compared with axenic cells on day 14, by which time the axenic culture was dying (the design notes record that axenic MED4 lost viability around day 11; in the low-nitrogen medium we take nitrogen exhaustion to be the cause), while the coculture survived. Because the axenic arm comes from a different run, this contrast also carries any run-to-run difference. A gene "down in coculture" on day 18 is therefore one that is higher in the nitrogen-starved control. The low-nitrogen starvation series from the same study compare each culture with itself in exponential growth: the coculture on day 11, the axenic culture on day 7.

**Testing whether the *hli* set responds.** For every experiment, time point and direction (up or down), we asked whether *hli* genes were over-represented among the genes the study called significant, using a one-sided Fisher exact test. Where a study reported every measured gene, the background was the set of measured genes. Several studies reported only the genes they found significant. For those, a gene absent from the table was taken as not significant, the whole genome served as background, and the test is marked approximate. Tests with fewer than 10 significant genes genome-wide in that direction were not run, because a handful of changed genes cannot show over-representation. Down tests were not run on one phage microarray table that lists only induced genes. Where more than 30% of all genes changed in one direction, the test was run but flagged, since nearly any gene set then overlaps the changed genes.

P-values were corrected for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, separately for each gene set across all tests. To give each experiment a single call per direction, we took its smallest time-point p-value, multiplied it by the number of testable time points, and corrected these values across experiments. All contrasts are read as treatment versus control, as the studies reported them. The corrections were computed before the two excluded tables were dropped and still include their tests; recomputing without them changes no call.

To see whether an *hli* response was specific or part of a general reaction, the same tests were run on ten reference sets from the Cyanorak functional categories. These were:
- ribosomal proteins;
- protein folding and protein degradation;
- adaptation to nitrogen, phosphorus and iron limitation;
- the light-adaptation category with the *hli* genes removed;
- photosystem I, photosystem II and CO2 fixation.

We also tested 200 random gene sets of the same size in each test, to estimate how often an arbitrary set reaches significance.

**Near-identical paralogs.** Many MED4 *hli* genes are near-identical copies. Four pairs (PMM0815/PMM1396, PMM0816/PMM1397, PMM0817/PMM1398 and PMM0818/PMM1399) had exactly the same fold change in every microarray table that listed both members, so each pair evidently shares one probe signal. In microarray tables that list only significant genes, usually only one member of a pair appears. Counting the other member as "not significant" would invent a difference that the platform cannot measure. We therefore treated it as undefined. As a sensitivity check, we repeated the set-level tests on the microarray tables with each pair counted once. RNA-seq tables, in which the pairs have different values, were left as reported.

**Gene-level patterns.** These analyses were exploratory and were added after the set-level results had been seen. Each gene's call in each experiment and time point was coded as up (+1), not significant (0), down (−1) or undefined. The 23 genes were then clustered by average linkage, using as distance the fraction of shared defined calls on which two genes disagree. We checked the stability of the clusters in two ways: with a correlation-based distance, and with the light-onset arrays removed.

For each gene we also recorded its domain and ontology annotations, its immediate genomic neighbours and whether another *hli* gene encodes an identical protein, and how widely its ortholog group is found among the 123 organisms in the knowledge graph (101 of them picocyanobacterial genomes), and in which *Prochlorococcus* clades (high-light or low-light, as recorded for each organism). Domain annotations, protein length and simple sequence composition were compared between the gene groups found by clustering, as an uncorrected screen of about 15 features. For the time courses we report, at each time point, the percentage of measured *hli* genes called up and called down, with the percentage of ribosomal-protein genes called down as a growth reference. Finally, we compared the strength of the *hli* response across studies with each study's background light intensity, using Spearman rank correlation. The time-course and light-level analyses are also exploratory.

All numbers reported here are produced by scripts in the analysis repository, and every figure is generated from saved tables.

## Results

**The *hli* genes are the light-onset responders.** In all four light-onset comparisons that could be tested, *hli* genes were strongly over-represented among the induced genes (per-experiment corrected p between 5 × 10⁻⁸ and 2 × 10⁻¹⁰). Blue, red and dim white light gave this result, and so did brighter white light. In each case 8 to 11 of the 11 *hli* genes listed in the study's table were induced, spread over 5 to 8 different ortholog groups. The green-light shift changed too few genes for a test. None of the ten reference sets was induced at light onset. Photosystem I genes went down in two of the six light-onset tests (Fig. 1).

Adding DCMU to illuminated cells changed few genes, and only three *hli* genes, all of them down. Constant darkness compared with constant light, with or without phage, produced no set-level *hli* change.

**The same genes respond to several stresses applied in the light.** *hli* genes were over-represented among induced genes in four of the five stresses (Fig. 1):
- **CO2 limitation:** both testable experiments (corrected p 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ and 5 × 10⁻¹⁰);
- **Iron starvation:** p 6 × 10⁻⁵;
- **Nitrogen limitation:** three experiments (p 5 × 10⁻¹² to 8 × 10⁻³);
- **Phage infection:** on the microarray (p 8 × 10⁻¹²).

There was no response to low salinity or phage lysate in the light, nor to phage lysate in the dark. The response also ran in reverse when a stress was relieved. *hli* genes went down when iron was added back to starved cells (p 9 × 10⁻⁶), and when cultures were sparged with air relative to time 0 (p 1 × 10⁻⁶). They were also induced when urea or cyanate replaced ammonium as the nitrogen source (p 8 × 10⁻¹¹ and 2 × 10⁻¹²). These comparisons were classed as non-stress, although cells on these alternative sources may be mildly nitrogen-limited.

Counting each paralog pair once did not change any test or experiment call; 20 tests were significant before and after. It did reduce the phage result, which rests on five independent signals rather than nine, but that result remained clear (p 1 × 10⁻⁶).

**Which genes carry the response: responders and quiet genes.** *This part of the analysis was exploratory, and was added after the set-level results had been seen.* The tests above treat the 23 *hli* genes as one set. Gene by gene, they are not one set. Clustering the genes on their calls in every experiment and time point gives four clusters (Fig. 2). Two of them, C3 and C4 (15 genes), go up together at light onset and under stress; we call them the *responders*. The other two, C1 and C2 (8 genes), change much less often; we call them the *quiet genes*, although two of them respond often enough to sit between the groups (below). Per gene, a median of 29% of the responders' stress and light-onset calls were up (27% on RNA-seq alone), against 2.5% (7%) for the quiet genes. The ranges touch, however: 17–45% for the responders and 0–20% for the quiet genes. Fig. 3b breaks the comparison down by condition.

The split follows one genomic feature (Fig. 3a). Fourteen of the 15 responders are multi-copy in MED4; none of the quiet genes is. Twelve responders lie next to another *hli* gene, in two tandem blocks (PMM0815–PMM0818 and PMM1396–PMM1399) and two pairs (PMM0689–PMM0690 and PMM1384–PMM1385), and ten have an identical copy elsewhere in the genome. No quiet gene has either. All responders belong to ortholog groups found only in *Prochlorococcus*, while the quiet genes include the four whose groups extend to *Synechococcus* and *Cyanobium*. These are not independent lines of evidence. A gene with an identical copy is multi-copy by definition. The four identical pairs share one probe signal on the microarrays, so their calls there agree mechanically. Genes in a tandem block are probably transcribed together. What the data show is that the multi-copy, clustered, *Prochlorococcus*-restricted *hli* genes respond together, and the single-copy genes mostly do not.

Domain and ontology annotations do not separate the groups: the chlorophyll a/b-binding domain is on 12 of the 15 responders and 5 of the 8 quiet genes. In the screen of about 15 annotation and sequence features, protein length did not differ (median 46 vs 58 amino acids). Only the fraction of charged residues differed nominally (median 0.15 vs 0.22 with identical copies counted once, p = 0.04, uncorrected).

**The responders.** These 15 genes carry the set-level response described above. In the heatmap they form one block of rows in which they go up together (Fig. 2). They were induced at light onset (35 of 55 defined calls; Fig. 3b), under CO2 limitation (22 of 37), nitrogen limitation (64 of 139) and iron starvation (7 of 33), and when urea or cyanate was the nitrogen source (13 of 20). After phage infection they were induced in 14 of 195 calls, 9 of them at the first hour on the microarray. They went down when iron was added back (7 of 11) or air was supplied (7 of 13). They did not change in the salt experiment, and rarely in the dark-phage experiment (2 of 45 calls down, both PMM0689). The six genes whose groups occur throughout *Prochlorococcus* had a median of 33% of calls up (37% on RNA-seq).

**The quiet genes.** As a group, these eight genes rarely responded to stress (16 of 102 nitrogen-limitation calls up) and almost never to light onset (3 of 40). Most of those up-calls come from two genes. PMM1128, alone in cluster C1, was up in 6 of 14 nitrogen-limitation calls and 2 of 5 light-onset calls (20% of its stress and light-onset calls; 40% on RNA-seq). PMM1390 was up in 7 of 14 nitrogen-limitation calls (17.5% overall). Both are single-copy and *Prochlorococcus*-specific, and both respond about as often as the least responsive responders. The other six are quiet throughout. They include the four genes whose ortholog groups are shared across nearly all picocyanobacteria in the knowledge graph, which were the least responsive of all (a median of 2.5% of calls up; 7% on RNA-seq), and two *Prochlorococcus*-specific single-copy genes, PMM1317 and TX50_RS09730.

**The groups help read the schedule-extension experiments.** In two of the four schedule-extension time points more than 30% of the genome changed, and at the set level the *hli* reading was unclear. Gene by gene, six responders (PMM0818, PMM1118, PMM1384, PMM1385, PMM1398 and PMM1399) were higher in the darker arm of both experiments: up when darkness was extended, down when light was extended. Two quiet genes, PMM0064 and PMM0093, rose in both experiments instead. The split is not clean. Two responders, PMM0690 and PMM1404, went down when darkness was extended (PMM1404 was down at all four time points), and the quiet gene PMM1390 followed the responder pattern. Opposing movements of this kind cancel at the set level.

**How firm the grouping is.** We re-clustered with a correlation-based distance and without the light-onset arrays. The responder / quiet split held for 20 and 21 of the 23 genes, respectively. In both cases the genes that moved were quiet genes joining the responder block: PMM1128, PMM1390 and TX50_RS09730 under the correlation distance; PMM1390 and TX50_RS09730 without the light-onset arrays. The boundary of the quiet group is therefore soft, and its core is the six genes that never responded. An earlier clustering split the responders in two. That split reflected which member of each identical paralog pair a study had happened to list, and it disappeared once the pairs were handled as described in Methods. Copy number, genomic arrangement and ortholog breadth are confounded: every gene in a picocyanobacteria-wide group is single-copy and stands alone in the genome, and every gene in a *Prochlorococcus*-wide group is multi-copy. These data cannot tell which of them matters.

**The timing of the response depends on the stress.** *Exploratory.* In the time courses, phage infection induced 9 of the 23 *hli* genes (five independent signals) one hour after infection, and none from two hours on (Fig. 4). Nutrient limitation produced a slow build-up instead:
- **Nitrogen deprivation:** no *hli* gene was induced in the first three hours. The response rose to 10 of 17 measured genes by 48 h, after ribosomal-protein genes had already fallen.
- **Iron starvation:** none of 19 measured genes was up at 12 h, and 6 of 19 at 48 h.
- **Months-long low-nitrogen coculture:** induction peaked at day 31 and declined by day 89.

The *hli* response is therefore not confined to the onset of a stress. It is brief when the stress is acute and builds slowly when nutrients run out.

**Background light level.** *Exploratory.* Across studies, the strength of *hli* induction rose with the background light intensity under which the experiment was run (Spearman ρ = 0.49, p = 0.010). The strength of ribosomal-protein repression rose with it to the same degree (ρ = 0.48, p = 0.05), which points to stronger responses overall in the brighter studies rather than something specific to *hli*. The 27 tests come from only 13 experiments at 6 light levels, so these p-values overstate the evidence. In addition, cells in each experiment were acclimated to that light, and both arms were compared at the same intensity. We therefore do not read background light level as a driver of the *hli* response (Fig. 5).

## Discussion

Statements tagged *[interpretation]* draw on general knowledge of photosynthesis rather than on the data analysed here.

**Do *hli* genes respond to light?** Yes. In the light-onset experiment, cells kept 5 h in the dark were moved into dim light (13 or 55 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹). The *hli* set was induced in all four testable comparisons, and none of the ten reference sets was (Fig. 1). The induction is carried by the responders: 35 of their 55 defined light-onset calls were up, against 3 of 40 for the quiet genes (Fig. 3b). These light-onset results were seen while the analysis was being planned, so they confirm the source study rather than test a prediction made beforehand. Adding DCMU to illuminated cells moved three responders down rather than up. *[interpretation]* DCMU blocks electron flow out of photosystem II, so this fits a signal from the redox state of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, as expected for a photoprotective response. The result rests on three genes.

The other light contrasts complicate the picture.
- **Schedule extension (Johnson et al. 2026, RNA-seq).** Six responders were higher in the arm kept in darkness: up when darkness was extended past dawn, and down when light was extended past dusk. That is the opposite of the light-onset direction, and two of these four time points are flagged because more than 30% of the genome changed. *[interpretation]* The responders are therefore linked to light but do not simply track it. A time-of-day gating of the response, or a dawn response that had already passed by the sampling time, could explain the reversal.
- **Constant darkness vs constant light (Thompson et al. 2016).** The two arms were run on separate days, after cells had been moved into darkness, and the tables were mostly too small for a set-level test. At the gene level, four responders were lower in the dark at 0.5 h in infected cells.
- **Light level.** The knowledge graph holds no MED4 comparison of two steady light levels in which *hli* genes were measured; the high- vs low-light proteome quantified no *hli* protein. The cross-study comparison in Fig. 5 relates response strength to each study's background light, but within each study both arms were at the same light.

So *hli* genes clearly respond to a shift into light. Whether their expression also depends on the steady light level remains open.

**Is the response a general stress response unrelated to light?** The same responders were induced by CO2 limitation, nitrogen limitation, iron starvation and phage infection, and they went down when iron was added back (Fig. 3b). Salt acclimation did not induce them. Two qualifications apply.
- **The CO2 contrasts are not simple induction.** The source study attributes the fall of *hli* in the air control to recovery from the handling stress of the time-0 sample, and reads the low-CO2 arms as *hli* staying at or above time-0 levels. "Up under low CO2" relative to air therefore partly means failure to recover rather than new induction.
- **The stress line is blurred.** Urea and cyanate, which we classed as non-stress, induced the responders about as strongly as the stresses did (13 of 20 calls).

So the response is broad, but these data cannot show that it is unrelated to light. Every stress that induced the responders was applied in the light. The only stress run entirely in the dark, phage lysate, did not induce them. That comparison is weak, however: the dark experiment has no 0.5 h sample, which is the only time point at which lysate in the light induced responders (5 genes), and at the matched times (2.5–8.5 h) neither condition induced *hli*.

*[interpretation]* One reading is that the responders sense an imbalance between absorbed light and its use, whether the imbalance comes from more light or from a smaller sink. CO2 and nutrient limitation, and the takeover of host metabolism by phage, all reduce how much of the absorbed light the cell can use. The time courses are consistent with this reading, but they fit a general stress response equally well:
- the rise under nitrogen deprivation after ribosomal-protein genes had fallen fits any programme that starts when growth stops;
- the brief, early response to phage is not obviously predicted by the sink reading, since phage takeover progressively removes the host's use of light;
- reversal on relief is expected either way.

Oxidative stress, which excess excitation produces, is a further candidate signal. So are sample-handling stress and phage-driven changes in host transcription. Telling these apart needs stresses applied at two or more light intensities, with both arms acclimated.

**Two kinds of *hli* genes.** The multi-copy *hli* genes, which are clustered in the genome and restricted to *Prochlorococcus*, carry the light and stress response. They correspond to the multi-copy, *Prochlorococcus*-specific *hli* types that Bhaya et al. (2002) and Lindell et al. (2004) associate with the family's expansion. The single-copy genes were mostly not induced. The four genes shared with *Synechococcus* and *Cyanobium* were rarely induced, but they carry the most down-calls: PMM1482, for example, went down under low CO2, early in nitrogen deprivation and when darkness was extended. Steglich et al. (2006) described five *hli* genes as constitutively expressed, and the knowledge graph links that statement to PMM0064, PMM0093, PMM0471 and PMM1390. This is consistent with our coding but is not independent support, because Steglich's arrays are part of our data and of the clustering. Moreover, PMM1390, constitutive under the light shifts, was strongly induced by nitrogen deprivation in another study (Tolonen et al. 2006); it is one of the two genes at the boundary of the groups. *[interpretation]* One possibility is that the conserved single-copy HLIPs have a constant role, for example in the assembly or repair of the photosystems, while the expanded *Prochlorococcus* family supplies an inducible photoprotective capacity. Two quiet genes, PMM0064 and PMM0093, rose when either the light or the dark period was extended, which suggests a different, schedule-linked control. These readings are exploratory: the grouping was found after the set-level results were seen, its boundary is soft (PMM1128 and PMM1390 sit between the groups), and copy number, genome arrangement and conservation cannot be separated in these data.

| Tolonen et al. 2006 (nitrogen) | Three *hli* genes among the most upregulated under nitrogen stress | *hli* set up under N deprivation and on urea and cyanate | Agrees at set level; the statement is attached to 10 MED4 genes, so we cannot tell which three |
| Thompson et al. 2011 (iron) | PMM1404 upregulated during iron stress and downregulated upon rescue | PMM1404 up at 48 h of starvation and down after rescue; set up, then down | Agrees |
| Bagby & Chisholm 2015 (CO2) | Six *hli* genes fell after time 0 in the air control (recovery from handling stress) and stayed at or above time 0 without CO2 | Set down in air vs time 0; set up under low CO2 vs air | Agrees |
| Thompson et al. 2016 (phage, light and dark) | *hli* genes enriched in the light, much more so in infected cells | Infected cultures: four responders higher in the light at 0.5 h; uninfected: no change; set level not significant | Agrees at gene level |
| Lindell et al. 2007 (phage) | Describes the phage's own *hli* gene | Host *hli* set up at 1 h after infection | Not comparable |

For Read et al. 2017, He et al. 2022, Weissberg et al. 2025 and Johnson et al. 2026 the knowledge graph records no statement about *hli* genes, and we have not checked the papers themselves. Our results there are *hli* induction under nitrogen depletion and in the months-long low-nitrogen series, no response to low salinity, and the schedule-extension pattern.

**Caveats.**
- **Platforms.** Microarray and RNA-seq tables were combined at the level of each study's significance calls; fold changes were never compared across platforms. Several microarray tables list only significant genes, so a gene's absence was read as "not significant", and those tests are approximate.
- **Identical paralogs.** Four pairs share one probe signal on the microarrays. The set-level results survive counting each pair once, but at the gene level, microarray calls for a pair are a single observation.
- **Exploratory analyses.** The gene grouping, the time courses, the light-level comparison and the conservation analysis were all added after the set-level results had been seen. The light-onset and DCMU results were seen during planning. All of these should be read as descriptions to test, not as tests.
- **Light designs.** The light-onset evidence comes from one study and is a shift from darkness into dim light, not excess light over an acclimated level. The constant dark vs light arms were run on separate days. The knowledge graph holds no steady-state light-level contrast in which *hli* genes were measured.
- **Other designs.** The CO2 study's time-0 sample carries handling stress. In the 2025 coculture experiment the axenic arm comes from a separate run, so that contrast includes a run-to-run difference. Urea and cyanate were classed as non-stress but induce the responders. Two tables were excluded: a coculture table with unreliable calls, and a phosphate table that holds too few genes.
- **Conservation.** Ortholog breadth, copy number and genome arrangement are confounded in MED4.
- **Source comparison.** Each comparison re-reads the source's own data. Several source statements are attached in the knowledge graph to more, or other, *hli* genes than they name: one Tolonen et al. sentence to 10 MED4 genes, one Bagby & Chisholm sentence to 13, and one Steglich et al. operon sentence to a gene probably matched by name only. Gene-level comparisons with the source text are therefore approximate.

**Suggested follow-ups.**
1. Apply nitrogen or CO2 limitation at two or more light intensities, with both arms acclimated, and test whether the responders' induction scales with light. This is the direct test of question 2. Darkness alone stops growth and would not separate the readings.
2. Compare steady light levels in acclimated MED4 with *hli* genes measured, to settle whether expression tracks light level.
3. Use RNA-seq time courses to separate the identical paralog pairs.
4. Ask whether the responder / quiet split holds in other high-light strains (part B of the original plan, dropped for time).
